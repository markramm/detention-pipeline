# Baker County: From Overflow Site to Florida's Last State Detention Hub

## The Fight

Baker County hosts two distinct ICE-linked detention operations that are frequently conflated in national coverage but should be tracked separately: (1) **Deportation Depot**, the GEO Group-managed, state-built facility at the former Baker Correctional Institution in Sanderson, opened July 2025 as a 1,300+ bed complement to Alligator Alcatraz; and (2) the **Baker County Detention Center** in Macclenny, a two-decade-old sheriff-run county jail operating under ICE IGSA DROIGSA-09-0038, which is the subject of extensive ACLU litigation over solitary-confinement abuse, medical neglect, and denial of counsel access (see [[baker-county-fl-macclenny-jail]]).

The structural story that emerged in this research pass: when Gov. DeSantis closed Alligator Alcatraz on June 25, 2026 after roughly 11 months and 21,000 processed deportations, he explicitly kept Deportation Depot open and framed it as the facility that "remains" — meaning Florida's state-level detention capacity did not shrink with the Everglades closure, it consolidated into Baker County. This directly matters for the neighboring Bradford County IGSA fight (see [[bradford-county-fl-douglas-building]]): DHS framed the Alligator Alcatraz closure as a "hurricane season precaution" and a handoff to permanent capacity, but Deportation Depot is not new permanent federal capacity — it's existing state capacity absorbing the load. Whether Bradford's Douglas Building proposal (still tabled as of July 2026) is actually needed as "successor capacity," as DeSantis implied, is undercut by the fact that Baker County is already absorbing the transferred population.

## Key Details

- **June 16-18, 2026**: DHS transfers all remaining Alligator Alcatraz detainees to "undisclosed locations," officially framed as a hurricane-season precaution; some go to Baker CI (Deportation Depot), others out of state (South Florida, California, Arizona, Louisiana, Texas)
- **June 25, 2026**: DeSantis formally announces Alligator Alcatraz closure after 11 months/21,000 deportations aided; confirms Deportation Depot in Baker County "will remain open"
- **Funding overlap**: Both facilities drew on the same $608M FEMA grant (awarded Oct 2025), which was frozen by an incomplete federal Environmental and Historic Preservation review from Dec 2025 until the Trump administration lifted the hold March 13, 2026 — Florida can now bill up to $249/detainee/night without completing the review, though the underlying NEPA litigation (Friends of the Everglades, Earthjustice, Miccosukee Tribe, Center for Biological Diversity) continues
- **Separate, unresolved thread — FDLE investigation**: Baker County Sheriff Scotty Rhoden (who runs the Macclenny IGSA jail, not Deportation Depot) has been under a Gov. DeSantis-ordered FDLE investigation since June 2024, reassigned to 4th Circuit State Attorney Melissa Nelson after the original prosecutor recused over a campaign endorsement conflict; an extension pushed the deadline to June 4, 2026. As of this research (July 2026) no public outcome has been reported — the investigation's substance has never been disclosed, and its status should be treated as pending, not closed.
- **ACLU campaign**: Active "Shut Down the Baker County Detention Center" petition targets the Macclenny jail specifically, not Deportation Depot — a distinction worth preserving in any future coverage since the two facilities have very different operators, contract structures, and complaint histories

## Why It Matters

Baker County is now doing double duty in Florida's detention infrastructure: it is simultaneously (a) the newest, most politically visible expansion node (Deportation Depot, explicitly positioned as Alligator Alcatraz's successor) and (b) the site of the state's most litigated, longest-running conditions-abuse record (the Macclenny IGSA jail). For the pipeline's Sabot-style targeting-pattern tracking, this is a useful counter-case to Bradford: Baker shows what happens *after* a facility is sited and built — sustained federal deportation throughput, an open-ended conditions-litigation tail, and reduced public visibility once the initial siting fight is over. It's the mature end of the pipeline that Bradford's still-tabled proposal represents the early end of.
